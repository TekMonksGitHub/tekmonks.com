## 2\. The Problem: Foreign Reverse-Proxy Networks in the Path of Government Traffic
### 2\.1 How Cloudflare-style architectures work
Cloudflare and similar services provide DNS + CDN + security by acting as a **reverse proxy**: a server that sits in front of your web server and forwards all user requests to it. 

Key implications:

- **All inbound traffic** (citizen to website) first terminates at the CDN’s edge servers.
- **TLS certificates** are often managed by the CDN, meaning decryption and re-encryption can occur on their infrastructure.
- **Requests are routed through a global network** of data centres, where traffic may traverse or terminate in multiple countries. 
- **Logs, analytics and caches** containing IPs, URLs, headers, cookies, and sometimes PII, are stored by the provider across its infrastructure.

In short: **for any government site fronted by such a service, citizen traffic is processed by a foreign network before it reaches the Indian server.**
### 2\.2 Today’s outage: A symptom of systemic dependence
On 18 November 2025, a major Cloudflare incident caused widespread 500-errors, with numerous high-profile sites (including X, Zoom and others) either partially or fully inaccessible. Reports show:

- **Thousands of outage reports** across multiple regions.
- Impact on **informational, government and social-media websites** relying on Cloudflare’s network. 

For any Indian Government service using this model, this means:

- A **foreign network failure** can become a **de facto denial-of-service** for Indian citizens.
- The department itself **does not fully control** its uptime; it is dependent on the global health of a private, foreign infrastructure.

## 3\. Indian Legal and Policy Landscape: Data, Sovereignty, and Control
### 3\.1 Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and draft Rules 2025
The DPDP Act 2023 is India’s cross-sectoral law governing personal data. It: 

- Requires **data fiduciaries** (including government entities) to implement “reasonable security safeguards” against breaches.
- Establishes obligations around **consent, purpose limitation, storage limitation, rights of data principals, and breach notification**.
- For **cross-border data transfers**, uses a **“blacklist” approach**: transfers are allowed unless a country/territory is specifically restricted by the Government of India. 

Even where cross-border transfers are formally permissible, data fiduciaries must:

- Ensure that personal data receives **comparable protection**, regardless of where it is processed.
- Be able to **demonstrate compliance** and respond to data principal and regulator queries about where and how data is processed.

**Issue with foreign reverse-proxies:**\
When every request to a government website is automatically routed via a foreign CDN’s global network, the department has to demonstrate:

- Where exactly the data (including meta-data and logs) is processed and stored.
- What safeguards exist when data may be processed in multiple jurisdictions.
- How to comply with potential **country blacklists** and sectoral restrictions in the future, if a PoP in a restricted territory is part of the provider’s routing.
### 3\.2 GIGW & Government hosting expectations
The **Guidelines for Indian Government Websites and Apps (GIGW)** and related documentation emphasise that: 

- Government websites should be in **.nic.in / .gov.in domains**.
- Websites should be **hosted in data centres in India**, with state-of-the-art security.
- Sites should undergo **security audits** and obtain conformity certifications (e.g. STQC / GIGW 3.0 compliance). 

In parallel, **MeitY-empanelled cloud service providers** must keep **data centre facilities and data functions within India**, ensuring **data residency** and IT Act compliance. 

**Issue with foreign reverse-proxies:**\
Even if the *origin* server is in a NIC or state data centre in India, the **effective hosting and termination point** visible to citizens is the foreign CDN. The real “front door” to the government service is:

- A domain whose DNS is controlled externally, and
- A reverse-proxy layer that may be **physically and legally outside India.**

This raises the question: *is the spirit of “hosted in data centres in India” met when all traffic is first processed by a global foreign network?*
### 3\.3 CERT-In Directions 2022: Logs and incident handling within India
CERT-In’s 2022 Directions significantly tightened cyber-incident obligations: 

- **Mandatory reporting** of specified cyber incidents within **6 hours**.
- Requirement to **maintain logs for 180 days within India** and furnish them to CERT-In when required.

If key access and security logs for government websites are held primarily in a **foreign CDN’s infrastructure**, there are practical questions:

- Are logs maintained **within Indian jurisdiction**, as required?
- How fast and completely can logs be furnished to CERT-In if the provider is foreign and geographically distributed?
- Who is responsible if log retention or format at the CDN layer does not meet CERT-In expectations?

## 4\. The Hard Questions Government Departments Must Ask
In light of today’s outage and the legal context above, every Indian government entity relying on Cloudflare or similar services should be able to answer, clearly and on record:
### 4\.1 “How can you route data via a US (or foreign) server?”
- Can you prove that **all citizen traffic and logs** stay within India, when the CDN’s design is inherently global? 
- Can you guarantee that **encryption keys are never used or stored outside India**?
- Have you documented this architecture in your **DPDP and CERT-In compliance posture**?
### 4\.2 “How can a global outage affect you, implying you don’t control your destiny?”
Today’s event shows that: 

- A misconfiguration or incident in a foreign CDN’s global control plane can take down Indian services instantly.
- Government departments may have **no way to override** or “route around” such an outage quickly.
- This contradicts the principle of **digital sovereignty** – the idea that India should be able to maintain its public services irrespective of private outages abroad.
### 4\.3 “How are you then compliant with Indian privacy and data laws?”
- Have you conducted a **formal Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** for routing citizen traffic via a foreign network?
- Can you map the CDN’s operations to **DPDP Act requirements** and the upcoming **DPDP Rules 2025**? 
- How do you ensure **cross-border transfers** align with future blacklists or sector-specific restrictions?
### 4\.4 “Should you not be using local and isolated servers inside India to stay safe, sovereign, and compliant?”
Given:

- GIGW’s emphasis on Indian data centres, 
- MeitY-empanelled cloud localisation requirements, 
- CERT-In’s log-localisation requirements, 

…there is a strong argument that **critical government websites should be fronted by Indian-resident, government-grade infrastructure**, not by a foreign global network.
